The platform and tooling map

Eight capability groups, 91 components. The test each one has to pass is simple: does engagement N+1 get cheaper than engagement N? Anything that doesn't compound across accounts is a tool an individual uses, not a platform the practice owns.

Orientation

How to read this map

Every component carries two classifications. Together they answer the two questions that actually matter when someone hands you a list this long: should we build this ourselves, and do we need it yet.

Investment posture

Build

No adequate off-the-shelf answer, or the thing itself is our differentiation. This is where engineering effort earns its return.

Wrap

A mature tool exists but needs a thin layer to fit our workflow, our ontology, or our audit requirements.

Buy

Commodity. Building it is waste. Pick a vendor, integrate, and spend the time saved on the build column.

The posture distribution is uneven by design and worth noticing. Group 6 is almost entirely buy — secrets management, CI/CD, infrastructure-as-code and incident tooling are solved problems, and a quarter spent building a feature-flag service is a quarter not spent on ontology. Group 5 is almost entirely build, because nothing off the shelf does entity resolution against a client's own domain model in a form you can hand to their people.

The boundary rule

Group 7 holds the reusable asset. Group 0 holds the live instance of it on a given engagement.

A discovery playbook is a Group 7 asset; the filled-in workflow map for one client is a Group 0 artefact. A requirement template lives in 7; the requirements register lives in 0. Without this rule the two groups duplicate each other endlessly, because one is organised by lifecycle stage and the other by asset type. Every Group 0 instrument should be seeded by a Group 7 asset, and every engagement should return something to Group 7 at close.

Sequencing

Build order, if you're starting from zero

The list is a destination, not a plan. Attempting it in parallel produces a platform organisation that occasionally does engagements — which is the failure mode this map exists to avoid.

FirstGroups 0, 1 and 2 at Tier 1. Observation instruments, a harness with permissions and approvals, and a trace and decision record. These three make a single engagement deliverable and defensible.
SecondGroups 3, 4 and 7 at Tier 1–2. Evals, connectors and the asset library. This is where compounding starts and where the margin on engagement five comes from.
ThirdGroups 5 and 6, plus Tier 3 throughout. Ontology depth and deployment substrate, driven by the account size and regulatory exposure you're actually selling into.

One standing rule for whoever owns the roadmap: nothing enters the build column without a named engagement that needed it. Every component here should be traceable to a moment where an FDE was slowed down or a client asked a question we couldn't answer. Platform work sourced from anywhere else is speculative, and speculative platform work is how this list becomes a five-year programme.
