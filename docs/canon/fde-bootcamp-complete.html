Discovery and the domain

Before anyone builds anything: how to recover the real workflow from the stated one, map an organisation, read its systems, and turn its vocabulary into a domain model. Every technique this week is platform-agnostic — the principles come from the best FDE practices in the industry, the tooling is whatever the engagement allows.

MD
The running client

Meridian Distribution

One fictional client carries the entire bootcamp. Everything you produce this week — the operating map, the exception inventory, the ontology — is the foundation Week 2 builds on and Week 3 deploys against. Treat it as cumulative: sloppy discovery now becomes your problem in ten days.

Meridian Distribution is a mid-size specialty distributor: ~2,400 employees, 11 warehouses, ~40,000 orders a month. On-time fulfilment sits at 81%; the COO has promised the board 93% within two quarters. The order desk is drowning in email — orders arrive from 40+ customer formats, half of them exceptions. Finance migrated to a new ERP eighteen months ago at a cost nobody will repeat. Sales runs a CRM that disagrees with the ERP about who the customers even are. A legacy AR system nobody will decommission holds nine years of history. And one senior order-desk analyst, there since the beginning, is the only person who knows how the ambiguous cases get routed.

Facilitators play five Meridian characters across the week: the COO (sponsor), the order desk analyst (operator and exception holder), the order desk manager (process owner), the platform lead (systems gatekeeper), and a finance systems manager. Each has a public position, a private reality, and unlock conditions. What they tell you depends entirely on how you ask.

The week in one sentence

By Friday you can walk into an organisation cold and leave with a defensible picture of how it actually works — which is the half of the job no platform does for you.

D2
Tuesday

Observation craft — the real workflow

The documented process is very rarely the real process. Today is the single most important day of the bootcamp: learning to recover what actually happens rather than what people say happens. It is the first of the three crafts an FDE practises on day one of every engagement — the interview craft, the modelling craft, and the scoping craft — and the other two are only as good as this one, because you can't model or scope a workflow you never actually saw.

BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:30Why "an email arrives" is never one stepDecomposition drill: 40+ senders, no two alike, payloads in PDFs and screenshots and forwarded threads, half of them exceptions, routing rules in one person's head. The happy path is the part the client already controls; the long tail is where you're needed.
10:45–12:15The observation methodInterview vs. observation and why the second recovers what the first cannot. The four behavioural tells: repeated tasks, copy-paste between tools, tab-switching, phone-out-mid-task. The question set: what's the most annoying part of your morning · can I watch you do it · what happens after this step · when did it last go wrong · how do you know where to look · what do you ignore.
13:15–16:00Simulation: the order deskLive role-play with the Meridian order-desk analyst (SCN-01 pattern). Nine discoveries are seeded behind unlock conditions; most only open if you ask to observe rather than interview. The facilitator will repeat the public position indefinitely if you don't change approach — that repetition is feedback.
16:15–17:30Debrief + exception inventoryGround truth revealed, discoveries scored by tier. Then the craft of the exception inventory as a first-class artefact: every deviation, its frequency, its current handling, and whose head holds the rule. This inventory becomes Week 2's eval golden set — quality here propagates forward.
Field rule of the day

Residents get the truth. Your badge and your contractor email address are your data-mining permits — you cannot survey your way to the real workflow.

Capture tooling (process mining, screen recording, meeting transcription) tells you where to look. It never substitutes for sitting beside the operator, because it records what fires, not why, and never what someone stopped mentioning because nobody listened last time.

The shape is the transferable asset; the tool is a detail of the account.

D5

Ontology — nouns, verbs and the canonical model

The most Foundry-shaped day of the programme, taught without Foundry. Every organisation is nouns and verbs — entities and operations — and the same real-world thing is usually named differently, at different grains, by every function. Modelling that once, canonically, is the highest-leverage act in the whole engagement.

BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:30The vocabulary collisionSales says account, finance says billing entity, delivery says client, engineering says org_id — one thing, four grains, zero maintained mappings. Why this breaks integrations weekly and why it's a feature of human organisations, not a bug. The four questions: which terms are overloaded, where are the integration points, what are the translation layers, which systems of record will never move.
10:45–12:15Canonical modellingEntities, attributes, relationships, events, actions and rules. Choosing the canonical grain — prefer the externally verifiable key. Building translation instead of forcing vocabulary change, and letting the model win adoption through the tooling rather than by mandate. Writeback: a model that can't act on the business is a glossary; actions and permissions make it operational.
13:15–16:00Simulation: four words for one customerSCN-03 against Meridian: 30 minutes each with sales ops, finance systems, the platform lead and a customer success manager. The seeded truth: the CRM–ERP integration breaks weekly on the 22% of customers with multiple legal entities, and three prior vendors fixed the failing job instead of the grain mismatch.
16:15–17:30Draft the Meridian ontologyCohort produces the draft ontology backlog: core entities with canonical grain and key, term mappings per function, candidate relationships, and the first ten business rules extracted from the exception inventory. This artefact is Week 2's foundation.
The strategic point

Users adopt your vocabulary along with your product. Whoever defines the terms owns the frame — and the linguistic foundation outlasts any single workflow you automate.

D6
Monday

Placement judgment — where intelligence belongs

This is the judgment that separates an FDE from an implementer, and the one the last cycle got wrong. Handing everything to the model produced hallucinations, blown budgets and a ~95% pilot failure rate. The correction: most of a good solution is still deterministic software.

Deterministic

Rules, thresholds, lookups, routing on structured fields. Exactly right every time, at negligible cost. Default here.

Model judgment

Classification of genuinely varied input, extraction from messy documents, summarisation. Surgical use only.

Human gate

Approval before anything irreversible or externally visible. Blast radius decides, not accuracy.

Leave alone

Too risky, insufficient ROI, already automated, or about to be replaced. A frequently correct answer.

BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:30The allocation gridEvery step in the operating map gets exactly one category and a written reason. Drill: of a typical ten-step workflow, perhaps three steps need genuine judgment; the rest are conditionals and API calls. Putting a model on all ten gets you 80% accuracy at forty times the cost of 99.9%.
10:45–12:15Risk and the calibration questionsBlast radius: what breaks, who notices, how fast, who gets blamed. Will I get a 2 a.m. call in six months · what's the trade-off for solving this fast · who owns it when I leave · is the volume high enough to matter. Why "is 88% accuracy good?" has no context-free answer.
13:15–15:00Design-time economicsProjecting unit cost before building: inference, compute, connector calls and human-gate load at expected volume. Model tiering — frontier to establish feasibility, then push subtasks down to smaller, cheaper models. This discipline is the difference between a pilot that scales and one that dies at budget review.
15:15–17:30Lab: the Meridian allocation gridEach trainee allocates the full order-desk workflow, prices it, and produces a ranked priority matrix — including at least two workflows recommended for no automation, with the client-facing rationale. Panel challenge: "why not just put a model on the whole thing?"
Graded skill of the day

Declining well. Telling a client that a workflow should not be automated — and keeping the relationship — is a demonstration of judgment, and clients read it as exactly that.

The sub-day rule, applied

Somewhere in the Meridian material is a four-hour fix hiding inside the big request. Trainees who spot it are expected to build and ship it today, before the MVP, and log what it bought them with the client characters. Escalating it into the MVP scope is the wrong answer. The harness — anatomy of a production agent

Full build day on the practice harness. The definition of working: the system completes the task when prompted badly — repeatedly, in the background, without depending on anyone crafting the perfect input. If it only works in your hands, you haven't built anything.

ComponentStandard
Agent loopBounded task, explicit completion criteria, iteration limits
ToolsNarrow, well-described, validated inputs and outputs — few tools used well
GuardrailsHard limits on scope, spend and irreversible actions, enforced in code, never only in the prompt
Structured outputDefined JSON schema, validated at every boundary — never free-form text between components
Context and memoryDeliberate: what persists, what's retrieved, what's discarded — grounded in the Meridian ontology
Human gateApproval queue wired for every action the allocation grid marked as gated
Audit trailEvery action, input, decision and confidence logged and inspectable — non-negotiable
BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:00Harness walkthroughThe practice harness and why one shared runtime beats every FDE rolling their own loop: shared guardrails, shared improvements, model independence. Where the client's stack plugs in and where it doesn't matter.
10:00–17:30Build labHeads-down implementation of the order-intake triage MVP against simulated Meridian data: 500 order emails across the real format distribution, including the exception classes from the Week 1 inventory. Facilitators float; nobody is rescued, everybody can ask.
Trust rule

If you cannot show the client exactly what the system did and why, they will never trust it — and untrusted systems don't get used, regardless of accuracy.

The audit trail is a software engineering problem. Solving it well puts you ahead of most of the market on its own.

D9
Thursday

Unhappy paths — building what's actually worth money

There is one way something goes right and a thousand ways it goes wrong. A system that handles only the happy path is worth approximately nothing, because the happy path is the part the client already controls. Today the Week 1 exception inventory earns its keep.

BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:15Failure enumerationSystematic technique: walk the exception inventory, walk each integration seam, walk each tool call. Timed drill — fifteen failure modes for a given step in ten minutes. Retry with backoff, graceful degradation, and explicit escalation when confidence is low.
10:15–16:00Hardening labFacilitators switch the data feed to hostile mode: malformed payloads, duplicate orders, the blank-date-epoch trap, a rate-limited endpoint, an exception class nobody inventoried. The MVP must degrade legibly — wrong answers routed to the human gate are a design success; silent wrong answers are the failure.
16:15–17:30Inject: the scope wobbleThe Meridian COO character appears mid-afternoon asking for "one small addition" that would double scope. Handling it is graded: the correct move protects the pilot charter while giving her a real path for the request.
The eighteen-month rule

Everything shipped today runs far longer than intended — every hack goes into production and stays there. Each artefact gets an explicit call at ship time: fold into the core offering, or discard by a named date. "Temporary" is a story, not a state. The rule's origin is literal: a Palantir FDE's "temporary" data-deletion script ran in production for eighteen months and is still remembered by his name. The equal and opposite scar is overengineering — the two-week configurable deduplication engine built for what needed a one-off SQL query, used exactly once. Calibration means collecting both scars, and today exists so you collect them cheaply here rather than expensively on an account.

Evals — turning non-determinism into evidence

An eval suite is a client deliverable in its own right, and often the artefact that closes the deal. It is how a probabilistic system becomes something an enterprise will put its name on.

BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:30The four testsDid it have the right data · did it take the required steps · does it match an expert · is it safe to act on. Routing to the human gate on the fourth is a design outcome, not a failure. Golden datasets from history; for subjective outputs, mining the existing corpus for implicit standards, then permanent human-in-the-loop feedback because evals never fully close subjective gaps.
10:45–12:15Agent readinessThe verifiability doctrine: autonomy is bounded not by model capability but by how well the environment can validate the work. Readiness as density of deterministic validation loops. The reframe delivered to clients: invest less in solving the problem, more in preparing the environment to verify it.
13:15–15:45Eval labBuild the golden set from the exception inventory, run the suite, produce the report in the practice format: 200 runs · 176 passed · 24 failed — 13 missing upstream data, 8 wrong record retrieved, 3 uninventoried exception class. Failures are the input to iteration, and showing them is what makes the passes credible. One fix-and-rerun cycle before close.
16:00–17:30Regression disciplineWiring the release gate: the suite re-runs on any change to prompt, model, tool, code or ontology, and blocks release below threshold. Human-review calibration: comparing agent output against expert judgment to know whether the evaluator itself can be trusted.

Into production — the autonomy ladder

Never flip a switch. The system earns autonomy one rung at a time, with an explicit exit criterion at each — and the shadow-mode harness is what turns each rung from a phase name into a measurement.

  1. Controlled environmentReal data in a sandbox. Exit when eval results are stable across representative volume.
  2. Shadow modeRuns beside the human on live traffic, acting on nothing. Both outputs logged; agreement and divergence computed. Exit when agreement is acceptable and every divergence is understood.
  3. Human approval on every actionAgent proposes, human approves. Exit when approval is a formality and rejections are rare and explicable.
  4. Autonomous with exception routingActs inside confidence thresholds, escalates outside them. Exit when escalation volume is stable and monitored.
  5. Autonomous with monitoringFull production. Metrics, KPIs and SLAs instrumented; a named person owns it.

10:45–12:15Shadow mode in practiceWiring the comparison harness: agent beside the simulated Meridian order desk, agreement rate computed per exception class. Reading divergence honestly — some disagreements mean the agent is wrong, some mean the golden set is, and telling them apart is the skill.

The product-return obligation

An engagement that produced a happy client and nothing reusable was a services project, not forward deployed engineering.

The fixed question at every close: what did we generalise, and where did it land? Contribution to the pattern library is a condition of finishing, not a virtue — and the practice measures it: at least one generalised asset (a connector, template, eval pack or module) shipped back to the library or platform within 90 days of engagement start. Productisation rate is the master metric of an FDE function. High billable utilisation with nothing flowing back is not success; it is the warning sign of a services shop in disguise.

D13
Wednesday

Value realisation and the executive readout

There are exactly three buckets a business cares about, and every deployed system is measured against all three.

Bucket 01

Cost savings. Hours recovered × loaded rate, minus run cost. Easiest to quantify, easiest to challenge — be conservative.

Bucket 02

Revenue uplift. Faster cycle time, higher throughput, capacity redeployed to revenue work.

Bucket 03

Risk mitigation. Error reduction × cost per error, compliance exposure, key-person dependency removed.

BlockSessionContent
09:00–10:30The ROI chainBaseline before you change anything — Week 1's KPI baseline was for this. Building the chain with visible arithmetic: this change reduces X, which moves Y, which is worth Z. Modelling partial automation, keeping human-gate cost in, including run cost. A number that survives the CFO beats an impressive one.
10:45–12:15The two rehearsalsRehearse as the engineer: architecture, decisions, trade-offs. Then as the VP: problem, outcome, evidence, risk. Different talks, same facts. Readout craft: recommendation in the first ninety seconds, a risk named before it's asked for, and the sentence the sponsor will repeat to their boss — written for them.
13:15–16:30Simulation: the eleven-minute readoutSCN-05 with the Meridian findings. Three executives: the warm-but-distracted COO who interrupts at minute four, the CFO who attacks the first number quoted, and the CTO who asks who owns this in eighteen months. Two rounds each; the second round is scored.
16:45–17:30DebriefWhy "what do I tell the board?" is the most important question in the meeting: the sponsor's upward narrative is the actual product being bought. What naming a risk unprompted buys you. What the interruption cost the people who lost the thread.

D14–15
Thursday–Friday

The capstone — a full engagement in miniature

Two days, a fresh client, no scaffolding. Everything the bootcamp taught, run end to end under the conditions a real engagement imposes: ambiguity, fragmented data, security constraints, a live client review, and a measured result. Trainees work in pods of two — a compressed version of the durable delivery unit in mature FDE functions: an FDE, a product manager and a platform or data engineer, owning one strategic account together — with individual accountability preserved in the scoring. One practice-level rule is taught here and repeated at certification: FDEs report to product or engineering with a dotted line to sales, never the reverse, because a function that answers to a quota bends every engagement toward the next close and the product-return loop dies.

The materials — deliberately imperfect

  • A new fictional client in a different vertical (rotates per cohort: a regional insurer, a healthcare network, a public-sector service desk), so nothing from Meridian transfers except the method
  • Ambiguous discovery inputs: a vague sponsor email, meeting notes that contradict each other, a stale process document, and access to three role-played characters with seeded unlock conditions
  • Fragmented data: exports from three systems that disagree about the core entity, one with a genuine quality landmine
  • A security constraint that invalidates the obvious architecture (e.g. a data-residency rule, or a regulated decision that demands a point-in-time record)
  • One inject per day, on the clock: a stakeholder conflict on Thursday, a scope-or-integrity pressure on Friday

The sequence

BlockExpected work
Thu AMDiscovery against the characters and materials. Operating map, exception inventory, stakeholder map, readiness read.
Thu PMAllocation grid, draft ontology for the contested entity, MVP spec with acceptance criteria, cost envelope. Sub-day fix shipped if spotted.
Fri AMBuild against the harness. Golden set from the exceptions. Eval run, one fix cycle, shadow comparison on the held-out slice.
Fri PMClient review with UAT: the characters run three real cases through the system live. Then the 11-minute executive readout with the measured outcome, taken from the eval and shadow evidence — not from hope.
What the capstone is actually testing

Not whether the system is impressive — whether the judgment chain holds: did discovery find the seeded truth, did the allocation follow the evidence, did the build match the allocation, did the evals measure the acceptance criteria, and did the readout claim only what the evidence supports. A modest system with an intact chain passes; an impressive demo with a broken chain does not.

Certification

Certification by shipped project, not by exam

The certification standard is borrowed from the best programmes in the field: you certify on what you shipped and how you defended it. The panel is a senior FDE, the practice lead, and one facilitator who played a capstone character.

The judgment chainEvery capstone artefact traces to evidence: map → grid → spec → build → eval → claim. Any broken link is a specific, named development area.
The behavioursObserved at least once across three weeks: chose observation over interview, declined an automation, shipped a sub-day fix, held a position under authority pressure, made an explicit fold-in/discard call.
The product returnAt least one contribution to the pattern library across the bootcamp — a recipe, a template, a scenario finding, an eval pack — accepted by the library owner.
  • Pass → enters shadow deployment on a live account, paired with an L3/L4 (weeks 9–14 of the practice programme)
  • Conditional → one named gap, one repeat simulation with a fresh finding set within two weeks
  • Redirect → strong on one side of the bar only; routed to the matching adjacent role with a written rationale — the average-of-both hire this outcome prevents is the most expensive mis-assignment the practice can make

Continuing development

After the bootcamp

Three weeks compresses the classroom and the simulation. It does not replace a real client — no simulation teaches what an actual account's politics, latency and ambiguity teach. Graduates follow the ramp progression the discipline's own veterans recommend:

  1. Observe live customer calls for the first two weeks on account — listening, logging, not speaking
  2. Shadow an implementation and a support escalation owned by a senior FDE
  3. Own one escalation end to end, with the senior as backstop
  4. Observe real users working on site — the bootcamp's Day 2, now with stakes
  5. Establish a recurring customer-learning loop — a living "what I've learned from customers" document, updated weekly with specific quotes, workflows and pain points, shared with the practice and feeding the pattern library on a cadence, not on inspiration

Last word

Every company can buy the same intelligence. The edge is where, how and why it gets applied — and that edge is a person who can walk into ambiguity, find what's true, decide what deserves to be built, build it so it survives, and prove what it was worth.

That person is what these three weeks exist to start building. The account finishes the job.
